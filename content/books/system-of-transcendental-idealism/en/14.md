# <em>Section One: On the Necessity and Character of a Supreme Principle of Knowledge</em>

<a id="s-0137"></a>
1\. It will be assumed meantime as a hypothesis, that there is indeed <em>reality</em> in our knowledge, and we shall ask what the conditions of this reality may be. —Whether there is <em>actually</em> reality in our knowledge will depend on whether these initially inferred conditions can be actually exhibited later on.

<a id="s-0138"></a>
If all knowledge rests upon the coincidence of an objective and a subjective (<em>Introd.</em> §1), the whole of our knowledge consists of propositions which are not <em>immediately</em> true, which derive their reality from something else.

<a id="s-0139"></a>
The mere putting-together of a subjective with a subjective gives no basis for knowledge proper. And conversely, knowledge proper presupposes a concurrence of opposites, whose concurrence can only be a <em>mediated</em> one.

<a id="s-0140"></a>
<em>Hence there must be some universally mediating factor in our knowledge, which is the sole ground thereof.</em>

<a id="s-0141"></a>
2\. It will be assumed as a hypothesis, that there is a <em>system</em> in our knowledge, that is, that it is a whole which is self-supporting and internally consistent with itself. —The sceptic denies this presupposition, like the first, and like the first it can be demonstrated only through the fact itself. —For what would it be like if even our knowledge, and indeed the whole of nature (for us) were internally self-contradictory? —Let us then <em>assume</em> merely, that our knowledge is a primordial whole, of which the system of philosophy is to be the outline, and renew our preliminary enquiry as to the conditions of such a whole.

<a id="s-0142"></a>
Now every true system (such as that of the cosmos, for example) must contain the ground of its subsistence within <em>itself;</em> and hence, if there be a system of knowledge, its principle must <em>lie within knowledge itself.</em>

<a id="s-0143"></a>
3\. <em>There can only be one such principle.</em> For all truth is absolutely <em>on a par.</em> There may certainly be degrees of probability, but there are no degrees of truth; one truth is as true as another. But that the truth of all propositions of knowledge is absolutely equal is impossible, if they derive their truth from different principles (or mediating factors); so there can only be one (mediating) principle in all knowledge.

<a id="s-0144"></a>
4\. This principle is the mediating or indirect principle in every science, but the immediate and direct principle only of <em>the science of all knowledge,</em> or transcendental philosophy.

<a id="s-0145"></a>
The task of establishing a science of <em>knowledge,</em> a science which puts the subjective first and foremost, immediately compels one towards a highest principle of all knowledge.

<a id="s-0146"></a>
All objections against such an <em>absolutely</em> highest principle of knowledge are already precluded by the very concept of transcendental philosophy. They arise merely from this, that the limited nature of the first task of this science is overlooked; it is a science which abstracts at the very outset from everything objective, and takes only the subjective into account.

<a id="s-0147"></a>
There is no question at all of an absolute principle of <em>being,</em> for against any such these objections are all valid; what we seek is an absolute principle of <em>knowledge.</em>

<a id="s-0148"></a>
But now it is obvious that if there were not an absolute limit to knowledge—<em>something</em> that, even without our being aware of it, absolutely fetters and binds us in knowledge, and that, <em>in the course of our knowing</em> never once becomes an object, precisely because it is the principle of all knowledge—then we could simply never arrive at knowledge, even of one solitary thing.

<a id="s-0149"></a>
The transcendental philosopher does not ask what ultimate ground of our knowledge may lie <em>outside</em> the same. His question is, what is the ultimate <em>in our knowledge itself,</em> beyond which we cannot go? He seeks the principle of knowledge <em>within knowledge;</em> (thus it is itself something that can be known).

<a id="s-0150"></a>
The claim that there is a highest principle of knowledge is not a <em>positive</em> claim, like that on behalf of an absolute principle of being, but <em>a negative, limiting</em> one, amounting merely to this: There is an ultimate of some sort, from which all knowledge begins, and beyond which there is no <em>knowledge.</em>

<a id="s-0151"></a>
Since the transcendental philosopher (<em>Introd.</em> §1) invariably takes only the subjective as his object, he likewise maintains that it is only subjectively, that is, <em>for us,</em> that there is a <em>primary knowledge</em> of some kind; whether, in abstraction from us, there is anything else whatever beyond this <em>primary</em> knowledge, he does not initially care at all, and the sequel must decide it.

<a id="s-0152"></a>
Now undoubtedly this <em>primary knowledge</em> is for us the knowledge of ourselves, or self-consciousness. If the idealist makes this knowledge into the principle of his philosophy, this is in accordance with the limited nature of his whole task, which has nothing for its object beyond the subjective element in knowledge. —That self-consciousness is the fixed point, to which everything is attached <em>for us,</em> is something that requires no proof. —But that this self-consciousness might merely be the modification of a higher being—(perhaps of a higher consciousness, and this of a higher one still, and so <em>ad infinitum</em>)—in a word, that even self-consciousness might still be something <em>explicable</em> as such, explicable by something of which we <em>can</em> know nothing, because the whole synthesis of our knowledge is first made precisely through self-consciousness—this is something that is of no concern to us as transcendental philosophers; for self-consciousness is not a kind of <em>being</em> for us, but a kind of <em>knowing,</em> and in fact the highest and most ultimate that there can ever be for us.

<a id="s-0153"></a>
To proceed further, it needs in fact to be proved, and has already been partly proved above (<em>Introd.</em> §1), that even when the <em>objective</em> is arbitrarily posited as primary, we still never get beyond self-consciousness. We are then either driven back endlessly in our explanations, from the grounded to the ground, or we must arbitrarily break the sequence, by positing an absolute that is both cause and effect—both subject and object—<em>of itself</em>, and since this is initially possible only through self-consciousness, by again positing a <em>self-consciousness</em> as primary; this occurs in natural science for which being is no more fundamental than it is for transcendental philosophy (see my <em>Sketch of a System of Nature-Philosophy</em>, p. 5 [<em>Sämtliche Werke</em>, ed. K. F. A. Schelling (1856-64), 3, 1-268]), and which posits its sole reality in an absolute that is both cause and effect of itself—in the absolute identity of the subjective and the objective, which we call nature, and which in its highest potentiality is again nothing else but self-consciousness.

<a id="s-0154"></a>
Dogmatism, for which <em>being</em> is fundamental, can explain things no otherwise than by an infinite regress; for the series of causes and effects, by which its explanation proceeds, could be closed only by something that is at once cause and effect of itself; but by that very fact it would be transformed into a science of <em>nature,</em> which itself again reverts on completion into the principle of transcendental idealism. (A consistent dogmatism is to be found only in Spinozism; but as a real system Spinozism again can endure only as a <em>science of nature,</em> whose last outcome is once more the principle of transcendental philosophy).

<a id="s-0155"></a>
It is evident from all this that self-consciousness circumscribes the entire horizon of our knowing even when extended into infinity, and that it remains in every direction the highest principle. Yet for present purposes we have no need of so commanding a thought, but only of reflection on the meaning of our first task. —The following argument will surely be found intelligible and plain to everyone.

<a id="s-0156"></a>
My only concern at the outset is to bring system into my knowledge itself, and to seek <em>within knowledge itself</em> for that by which all individual knowing is determined. —But now undoubtedly that which determines everything in my knowledge is the knowledge <em>of myself.</em> — Since I seek to ground my knowledge only <em>in itself,</em> I enquire no further as to the ultimate ground of this primary knowledge (self-consciousness), which, if it exists, must necessarily lie <em>outside</em> knowledge. Self-consciousness is the lamp of the whole system of knowledge, but it casts its light ahead only, not behind. —Even admitting that this self-consciousness were merely the modification of a being independent of it, a thing that no philosophy, to be sure, can render intelligible, it is no kind of being for me at present, but rather <em>a kind of knowledge</em>, and <em>only in this capacity do I consider it here.</em> Owing to the limitations of my task, which endlessly pens me back into the circle of knowledge, it becomes for me an autonomous and absolute principle—not of all being, but of <em>all knowledge,</em> since <em>all</em> knowledge (and not only my own) must start from it. —That knowledge as such, and in particular this <em>primary knowledge,</em> is dependent on something existing independently thereof, has yet to be proved by any dogmatist. Till now it remains just as <em>possible,</em> that all existence is merely the modification of a cognition, as that all cognition is merely the modification of an existent. — But yet disregarding entirely, and quite apart from the question whether it is existence that is necessary as such, and knowledge merely the accident thereof—<em>for our science</em> knowledge is for this reason autonomous, that we have regard to it solely as it is grounded <em>in itself,</em> that is, insofar as it is purely subjective.

<a id="s-0157"></a>
<em>Whether it is absolutely</em> autonomous can be left undecided, until such time as the science itself has determined whether anything whatever can be thought, which is not to be derived from this knowledge itself.

<a id="s-0158"></a>
Against the task itself, or rather against the <em>definition</em> thereof, the dogmatist can offer no objection, if only because I quite freely restrict my concern, and am only unable freely to <em>extend</em> it to something which, as will be evident in advance, can never fall within the sphere of my knowledge, such as an ultimate ground of knowledge beyond all knowledge. —The only possible objection to our procedure is that the task so defined is not a philosophical task, and its outcome not philosophy.

<a id="s-0159"></a>
But what philosophy <em>may be,</em> is precisely the question that has not so far been agreed upon, and whose resolution can only be the outcome of philosophy itself. <em>That</em> the accomplishment of this task is philosophy, can be decided only by the fact itself, <em>in that by achieving this task we simultaneously solve all problems whose solution has hitherto been sought in philosophy.</em>

<a id="s-0160"></a>
We thus maintain, with no less right than the dogmatist in maintaining the opposite, that what has hitherto been regarded as philosophy is possible only as a science of knowledge, and has <em>knowledge,</em> not <em>being</em> as its object; and that its principle, likewise, can be no principle of being, but only a principle of knowledge. —Whether we shall have more success in getting from knowledge to being, in deriving everything objective from a knowledge previously <em>assumed</em> as autonomous only for purposes of our science, and in thereby raising it to absolute independence—whether we shall do better in this than the dogmatist does in the opposite endeavor, of bringing forth knowledge from a being assumed as independent—the sequel must decide.

<a id="s-0161"></a>
5\. The first task of our science is to discover whether a passage can be found from knowledge as such (so far as it is an act) to the objective element therein (which is no act, but a being or subsistent); this task already postulates the autonomy of knowledge, and prior to the attempt there can be no objection lodged against it.

<a id="s-0162"></a>
The task itself therefore postulates at the same time that knowledge has an absolute principle <em>within itself,</em> and this principle lying within knowledge itself <em>is likewise to be the principle of transcendental philosophy as a science.</em>

<a id="s-0163"></a>
But now every science is a body of propositions under a <em>determinate form.</em> So if the entire system of science is to be based on this principle, it must not only determine the <em>content,</em> but also the <em>form</em> of this science.

<a id="s-0164"></a>
It is generally assumed that philosophy possesses a characteristic form, which we call the systematic form. To presuppose this form without deducing it is acceptable in other sciences, which already presuppose the science of sciences, but is not so in that science itself, which has as its object the very possibility of form as such.

<a id="s-0165"></a>
What is <em>scientific form</em> as such, and what is its origin? The science of knowledge must answer this question for all other sciences, — But this science of knowledge is itself already a <em>science,</em> and would thus require a science of knowledge concerning itself; but this too would be a science, and so <em>ad infinitum.</em> The question is how we are to account for this circle, since it obviously cannot be resolved.

<a id="s-0166"></a>
This circle unavoidable to science can have no explanation unless its original source lies in knowledge itself (the object of the science), in the following fashion: that <em>the original content</em> of knowledge presupposes the <em>original form,</em> and conversely, <em>the original form of knowledge</em> presupposes its original content, and both are mutually conditioned by each other. —For this purpose we should require to discover in the intellect itself a point at which, by one and the same indivisible act of primordial cognition, both content and form are generated. The task of finding such a point would be identical with that of discovering the principle of all knowledge.

<a id="s-0167"></a>
<em>The principle of philosophy must thus be one in which content is conditioned by form, and form in turn by content</em>—not the one presupposing the other, but each in reciprocity.—Among other arguments against a first principle of philosophy, the following is also employed. The principle of philosophy must admit of being expressed in a fundamental proposition: this must assuredly be not just a formal, but a material proposition. But now every proposition, whatever its content, falls under the laws of <em>logic.</em> Hence every material principle, merely by being such, presupposes higher principles, namely those of logic. — Nothing is wanting to this argument, save that it also be reversed. Let us consider any formal proposition, say, <em>A</em> = <em>A,</em> as the highest; the logical element in this proposition is merely the form of identity between <em>A</em> and <em>A;</em> but where, then, do I get <em>A</em> itself from? <em>If A</em> exists, it is equal to itself; but where does it come from? This question can assuredly be answered, not from the proposition itself, but only from a higher one. The analysis <em>A</em> = <em>A</em> presupposes the synthesis <em>A.</em> So it is evident that no formal principle can be thought without presupposing a material principle, or a material without presupposing a formal one.

<a id="s-0168"></a>
From this circle, that every form presupposes a content, every content a form, there is no escape whatever, unless some proposition can be found in which form is reciprocally conditioned and made possible by content, and content by form.

<a id="s-0169"></a>
The first mistaken assumption of the above argument consists, therefore, in taking the principles of logic to be <em>unconditioned,</em> that is, derivative from no higher propositions. —But now the principles of logic arise for us in this way only, that we turn what in other propositions is merely form into the actual content of the principles in question; thus logic can only arise as such by abstraction from determinate propositions. If it arises in a <em>scientific</em> manner, it can do so only by abstraction from the <em>highest</em> principles of knowledge, and since these, as principles, <em>themselves</em> on the other hand <em>already</em> presuppose the logical form, they must be such that in them <em>both</em> factors, the form and the content, reciprocally condition and involve each other.

<a id="s-0170"></a>
But now this abstraction cannot take place until such time as these highest principles of knowledge are established, and the science of knowledge is itself brought into existence. This new circle, that the science of knowledge is at once the foundation of logic, and yet has to be brought about in accordance with logical laws, is to be accounted for on the same lines as that exhibited earlier. Since, in the highest principles of knowledge, form and content are conditioned by each other, the science of knowledge must be at once the law and the most perfect embodiment of scientific form, and be absolutely autonomous in both form and content alike.
