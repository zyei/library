# <em>Section Two: Deduction of the Principle Itself</em>

<a id="s-0171"></a>
We are speaking of a deduction of the highest principle. It cannot be a question of deriving it from one still <em>higher,</em> and certainly not of a proof of its <em>content.</em> The proof can proceed only upon the <em>dignity</em> of this principle, or upon proving that it is the <em>highest,</em> and possesses all those characteristics which appertain thereto.

<a id="s-0172"></a>
This deduction can be carried out in many different ways. We adopt that which, being the easiest, allows us at the same time to perceive most immediately the true meaning of the principle.

<a id="s-0173"></a>
1\. That knowledge as such is possible—not of this or that particular thing, but of anything, be it only the knowledge that we know nothing, is admitted even by the sceptic. If we know anything at all, then this knowledge is either conditioned or unconditioned. —Conditioned?—we know a thing thus, only because it is connected with something unconditioned. So we arrive in any case at an unconditioned knowledge. (That there must be something in our knowledge, which we do not in turn know from some higher thing, has already been shown in the preceding section).

<a id="s-0174"></a>
The question is thus simply, what it is that we unconditionally know.

<a id="s-0175"></a>
2\. I know unconditionally only that of which the knowledge is conditioned solely by the subjective, not by anything objective. —Now it is claimed that only a knowledge expressed in <em>identical</em> propositions is conditioned by the subjective alone. For in the judgement <em>A</em> = <em>A</em> there is a total abstraction from the content of the subject, <em>A.</em> Whether <em>A</em> as such has reality or not is a matter of entire indifference for this knowledge. And so, if complete abstraction is made from the <em>reality</em> of the subject, <em>A</em> is considered simply insofar as it is posited <em>in us, presented</em> by us; whether this presentation corresponds to anything outside us is simply not asked. The proposition is evident and certain, quite regardless of whether <em>A</em> is something really existing, or merely imagined, or even impossible. For it says no more than this: in thinking <em>A,</em> I think nothing else but <em>A.</em> The knowledge in this proposition is thus conditioned purely <em>by my thinking</em> (the subjective), that is, as explained above, it is <em>unconditioned.</em>

<a id="s-0176"></a>
3\. But in all knowledge an <em>objective</em> is thought of as coinciding with the subjective. In the proposition <em>A</em> = <em>A,</em> however, no such coincidence occurs. Thus all fundamental knowledge advances beyond the <em>identity</em> of thinking, and the proposition <em>A = A</em> must itself presuppose such knowledge. —Having thought <em>A,</em> I admittedly think of it as <em>A;</em> but how, then, do I come to think <em>A</em> in the first place? If it is a concept freely engendered, it begets no knowledge; if it is one that arises with the feeling of necessity, it must have objective reality.

<a id="s-0177"></a>
Now all propositions in which subject and predicate are linked, not <em>by the mere identity of thinking,</em> but by something alien to the thought and distinct from it, are called <em>synthetic;</em> and if so, the whole of our knowledge consists of nothing but synthetic propositions, and only therein do we find true knowledge, that is, a knowing that has its <em>object</em> outside itself.

<a id="s-0178"></a>
4\. But now synthetic propositions are not <em>unconditioned,</em> self-evidently certain, for this is the case only with identical or analytic propositions (cf. 2 above). So if there is to be certainty in synthetic propositions—and thereby in all our knowledge—they must be traced back to an <em>unconditional certainty,</em> that is, to the <em>identity of thinking as such,</em> which is, however, a contradiction.

<a id="s-0179"></a>
5\. This contradiction would be soluble only <em>if some point could be found in which the identical and the synthetic are one, or some proposition which, in being identical, is at once synthetic,</em> and <em>in being synthetic, is at once identical.</em>

<a id="s-0180"></a>
In every synthetic judgement, <em>A</em> = <em>B,</em> a wholly alien objective coincides with a subjective; the predicate, the concept, always stands here for the subjective, and the subject term for the objective; and how we can attain to certainty in regard to such propositions is unintelligible,

<a id="s-0181"></a>
a) unless something, as such, is <em>absolutely true.</em> For if our knowledge involved an endless regress from principle to principle, then in order to arrive at that feeling of compulsion (the certainty of the proposition), we should have, unconsciously at least, to run through that unending series backwards, which is obviously absurd. If the series is genuinely without end, there can be no way of running through it. If it is not, then there is something absolutely true. —If there is such, then our whole knowledge, and every single truth in what we know, must be involved with that absolute certainty; <em>the covert feeling</em> of this connection is responsible for that sense of compulsion we have in taking any proposition to be true. —It is the task of philosophy to resolve this covert feeling into overt concepts, by exhibiting the connection in question, and the major linkages therein.

<a id="s-0182"></a>
b) This absolute truth can only be an <em>identical</em> piece of knowledge; but now since all true <em>knowing</em> is synthetic, the absolute truth, for all it is an identical cognition, must necessarily also be at the same time a synthetic one; so if there is such a truth there must also exist a point at which the synthetic springs directly from the identical cognition, and the identical from the synthetic.

<a id="s-0183"></a>
6\. In order to solve the problem of finding such a point, we must undoubtedly enter more deeply into the contrast between identical and synthetic propositions.

<a id="s-0184"></a>
In every proposition two concepts are compared together, that is, they are either set equal or unequal to each other. Now in identical propositions <em>the thought</em> is compared merely <em>with itself.</em> —The synthetic proposition, on the other hand, goes beyond the <em>mere</em> thought; in thinking the subject of the proposition, I do not also think the predicate; the latter is <em>annexed</em> to the subject. Thus the object here is not <em>merely</em> determined by the thought of it; it is regarded as <em>real,</em> since anything is real that cannot be brought about <em>merely</em> by thought.

<a id="s-0185"></a>
Now if an identical proposition is one in which concept is compared only with concept, while a synthetic proposition is one in which the concept is compared with an object distinct from itself, the task of finding a point at which identical knowledge is at the same time synthetic amounts to this: <em>to find a point at which the object and its concept, the thing and its presentation, are originally, absolutely and immediately</em> one.

<a id="s-0186"></a>
That this task is identical with that of finding a principle of all knowledge, can be still more briefly shown as follows. —There is absolutely no explaining how presentation and object can coincide, unless in knowledge itself there exists a point at which both <em>are originally</em> one—or <em>at which being and presentation are in the most perfect identity</em>

<a id="s-0187"></a>
7\. Now since presentation is the subjective, while being is the objective, the task, in a nutshell, consists of <em>finding the point at which subject and object</em> are <em>immediately</em> one.

<a id="s-0188"></a>
8\. By this even more exact delimitation of the problem, it is now as good as solved. This unmediated identity of subject and object can exist only where the <em>presented</em> is at the same time <em>that which presents,</em> where the <em>intuited</em> is also the <em>intuitant.</em> —But this identity of presenter and presented occurs only in <em>self-consciousness;</em> it is here, therefore, that the desired point has been found.
